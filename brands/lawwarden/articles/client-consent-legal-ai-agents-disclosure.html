Client consent for legal AI agents: what to disclose before an agent touches a matter

Published 2026-10-30T00:00:00-04:00 ·

The integration of legal AI agents into law practice is no longer a futuristic concept but a present-day reality. These sophisticated tools, such as LawWarden, offer unprecedented efficiencies in document review, legal research, contract analysis, and predictive outcome modeling. However, their use introduces novel ethical and professional obligations, chief among them being the imperative of informed client consent. Simply using AI is not a mere internal business decision; it is a method of practicing law that directly impacts the client's matter and their confidential data. Therefore, obtaining explicit consent is not just a best practice—it is a fundamental component of ethical representation in the modern era.

Why Consent Belongs in the Engagement

Informed consent is the bedrock of the attorney-client relationship, rooted in the core duties of communication (Model Rule 1.4) and confidentiality (Model Rule 1.6). Clients have a right to understand the means by which their legal services will be delivered, especially when those means involve entrusting their sensitive information to an artificial intelligence. Proactively seeking consent demonstrates transparency, builds trust, and manages client expectations. Crucially, it also serves as a critical risk management tool for the firm. It mitigates potential future disputes where a client might claim they were harmed or surprised by the use of an AI tool they did not authorize or understand. Embedding this discussion in the initial engagement process frames the use of AI as a value-added service, aligning the client with your firm's innovative approach to achieving their objectives efficiently and effectively.

What to Disclose for Informed Consent

True informed consent requires a clear and comprehensible explanation of what the client is agreeing to. Vague language is insufficient. The disclosure should cover several key areas:

Tasks the Agent Will Perform: Specify the AI's role. Will it be used for initial document review in discovery, contract clause extraction, legal research memo drafting, or predicting litigation outcomes? Avoid jargon; describe the function in plain language.

Data the Agent Will See and Process: Be explicit about the scope of client data that will be input into the AI. This could include case strategy memos, deposition transcripts, contracts, financial records, or other highly confidential information. The client must know what of theirs is being exposed to the system.

Where Data Goes (Hosting and Vendors): Disclose whether the AI is a first-party tool developed in-house or a third-party application (like LawWarden). If it's a third-party service, explain where and how the data is hosted (e.g., on the vendor's secure cloud servers) and affirm the vendor's contractual obligations to maintain confidentiality and data security standards.

Human Supervision: Clearly state that the AI is a tool aiding lawyers, not replacing them. Explain the level of human attorney oversight involved. Will an attorney review and verify all of the AI's output before it informs legal strategy or is filed with a court? This assurance is vital.

Inherent Limits and Risks: Acknowledge the limitations of AI. Disclose that while powerful, AI can make errors, generate hallucinations (confidently stated false information), or possess training data biases. Explain the firm's protocols for vetting and verifying the AI's work to catch and correct any such issues.

Consent Wording Points for the Engagement Letter

The engagement letter is the ideal place to document consent. The language should be clear, conspicuous, and affirmative. Avoid burying it in a long terms-of-service document. Consider a dedicated section or paragraph titled "Use of Artificial Intelligence" or "Technology in Service Delivery." The wording should be active, such as "We use AI tools to enhance our efficiency and analysis...", followed by a concise summary of the key disclosures (tasks, data, vendor, supervision). Crucially, it must include an explicit grant of permission: "By signing below, you consent to our use of these AI tools in your matter as described above."

Matter-by-Matter Versus Firm-Wide Consent

A blanket, firm-wide consent obtained once is unlikely to be ethically sufficient for all matters. The type of AI used and the data it requires can vary dramatically between a corporate transaction and a sensitive divorce proceeding. The gold standard is matter-specific consent, where the use of a particular AI agent is discussed and approved for the unique context of that case. However, a hybrid model may be practical. A firm-wide clause can disclose that the firm may use AI tools generally and commit to obtaining the client's specific, express consent before deploying an AI agent on their matter if its use involves heightened sensitivity or risk. This approach keeps the client generally informed while reserving deeper conversations for when they are most needed.

When to Re-consent

Consent is not a one-time event. The obligation to communicate continues. A firm should seek renewed consent if there is a material change in the way AI is being used. This includes: adopting a new AI agent with significantly different functionality or data security policies; expanding the AI's role to handle more sensitive data or perform more complex tasks than initially disclosed; or if a previously undisclosed risk or limitation of the tool becomes apparent. Re-consenting demonstrates ongoing respect for the client's autonomy and the fiduciary duty of continuous communication.

Filing the Consent Record

As with any critical ethical safeguard, documentation is paramount. The signed engagement letter containing the consent clause must be securely stored in the client's file. For verbal consent (which may be necessary in some situations but is less defensible), a detailed contemporaneous memo-to-file must be created, noting the date, time, what was explained, and the client's agreement. This record is the firm's first line of defense in demonstrating that it fulfilled its ethical duties should the use of AI ever be called into question by a client or a bar authority.
